## 🎙️ Sentiment Read

The tone from the Fed's public communications has turned **hawkish** relative to a few weeks ago, anchored to specifics rather than a vague vibe shift:

- Chair Warsh's Jackson Hole framework explicitly discarded the practice of naming data thresholds that would trigger a hike, forcing markets to interpret each data point (JOLTS, ISM, Friday's payrolls, and CPI around Sept. 10) through his subjective read of whether inflation is moving toward 2% "clearly and at sufficient speed." That is itself a hawkish structural change — it removes the Fed's self-imposed guardrails against surprising markets.
- Warsh's decision to sideline the Fed's AI/productivity task force from near-term policy closed off what had been read as a possible dovish escape hatch (using AI-driven disinflation as a reason to hold off on hikes).
- Barr's Sept. 1 remarks ("act decisively to raise rates" if inflation doesn't moderate) reinforce that the hawkish tilt is not confined to Warsh — though Barr also left the door open to "take more time" if data cooperates, and notably did not join the three governors who dissented in favor of a hike at the July 29 meeting (described by multiple outlets as the most hawkish FOMC dissent in nearly a decade).
- Countervailing signal: Sept. 1's JOLTS and ISM headline both came in slightly softer than consensus, and analysts are split on how much weight to give the hawkish rhetoric — Navy Federal's Heather Long said Warsh "opened the hike door" but expects the actual move (if any) in October or December rather than September; TradeStation's David Russell argued Warsh offered "lip service to price stability" without committing to a near-term timeline. BMO's Vail Hartman took the more hawkish read, calling Jackson Hole "deliberately hawkish."

Net: communications point toward a live (not settled) possibility of a September hike — a scenario that would have seemed unlikely a month ago — driven by inflation that has stalled well above target, tariff and Middle East-linked cost pressures, and a Fed chair less willing than his predecessor to pre-commit to easing.

That would be a more benign outcome for equities than a straightforward parallel shift higher in yields. *(Commentary, not certainty.)* Source: [Tech Times](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/326153/20260901/fed-rate-hike-68-odds-opens-five-day-window-variable-rate-borrowers.htm)
- **Oil/energy-linked inflation is an added complication.** The resumed U.S.-Iran conflict pushed Brent crude above $85/barrel and is cited by ISM respondents as a direct driver of the elevated Prices Paid reading — a supply-side inflation source the Fed's rate tool addresses only indirectly, which raises the risk of policy being "wrong-footed" in either direction depending on how the conflict evolves. This is a genuine analytical uncertainty, not a call on markets.
- **Credit-sensitive/variable-rate borrowers face the most mechanically direct impact.** The prime rate (currently 6.75%) would rise to 7.00% on a 25bp hike, directly raising costs on credit cards, HELOCs, and adjustable-rate mortgages; fixed-rate mortgage borrowers are more insulated and depend on the 10-year's path rather than the Fed funds rate itself.
