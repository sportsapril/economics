## 🎙️ Sentiment Read

The Fed's public communication is currently split between two identifiable poles, and that split — not any single data point — is the dominant story right now:

- **Hawkish pole (Warsh):** The new chair's Jackson Hole speech was explicitly framed around inflation control ("the Fed's predominant focus right now should be on prices") and broke with recent practice by moving away from standing forward guidance. Reporting characterizes this as a deliberate shift toward a "quieter," more inflation-focused central bank, and it is what drove hike odds from roughly one-in-three to well above 50% within days.
- **Data-dependent/more patient pole (Waller):** Waller's Sept. 3 remarks explicitly conditioned his vote on the August CPI/PCE prints due before the meeting, and he would prefer to hold if disinflation (three-month core trend down from 4.76% to 3.05%) continues. He also downplayed the reliability of the "nonmarket services" component driving recent PCE prints.

Net effect: this is not a settled hawkish or dovish signal — it's a live, first-real-test-of-the-new-chair situation, with the August CPI report (due Sept. 11, before the meeting) likely to be the single biggest swing factor for the Sept. 16 decision.

## 📊 Implications for the Broad Stock Market

*(The following is commentary/analysts' opinion, not fact — attributed accordingly.)*

- **A hike is not fully priced and would be a real surprise to some.** With odds hovering near 50-60%, a hike is a "live" outcome rather than a formality, meaning volatility around the Sept. 16 decision is plausible in either direction. JPMorgan's David Kelly has argued the market may have gotten ahead of itself on hike odds, noting "little in the labor market to suggest inflationary trouble ahead," while Federated Hermes' R.J. Gallo has leaned toward expecting a hike. (CNBC)
- **Historical pattern on first hikes after a long pause is initially negative, then positive.** Per data compiled by Carson Investment Research (cited by Motley Fool), the five prior quarter-point "first hikes" since 1990 were followed by S&P 500 losses one month later 100% of the time, and losses three months later 80% of the time (average -2.7%). But looking out a full year, the index was higher 100% of the time (average +12.5%). This is commentary/historical pattern, not a prediction.
- **AI/growth-stock exposure is the specific worry cited by commentators.** Because much of the current market cycle is tied to debt-financed AI infrastructure buildout, a rate hike raising borrowing costs is flagged by Motley Fool and others as a specific channel that could disproportionately pressure high-multiple growth/AI names relative to value.
- **Valuation backdrop adds to the sensitivity.** Separate Motley Fool commentary (Sept. 7) argues broad-market valuations are at levels last seen near the dot-com peak — cited here as commentary/opinion, underscoring why rate-path surprises could have an outsized market reaction either way.
- **Yield curve is not signaling acute stress.** The 2s10s spread (~+43bp) is positive, i.e., normally sloped rather than inverted, which historically has not been the recession-warning signal that an inverted curve represents — though this is a snapshot, not a forecast.
