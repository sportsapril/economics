## 🏛️ What's New From the Fed

**Minutes of the July 28–29 FOMC meeting were released Wednesday, August 19 at 2:00 p.m. ET** — this is the single most consequential Fed development in the last 24 hours. The minutes confirmed the July meeting produced the Committee's most fractured vote in nearly a decade: the FOMC held the target range at 3.50%–3.75% by a 9–3 vote, with three regional Reserve Bank presidents — Beth Hammack (Cleveland), Neel Kashkari (Minneapolis), and Lorie Logan (Dallas) — dissenting in favor of a 25bp hike. It's the first time since September 2016 that three policymakers dissented in the same (hawkish) direction.

Key specifics from the minutes:
- "Many participants assessed that policy tightening would likely be necessary if inflation did not decline."
- Some participants said current financial conditions "might not currently be sufficiently restrictive to facilitate a return of inflation to 2 percent."
- The dissenters argued a July hike "would likely help forestall the need for a steeper and potentially more costly sequence of tightening moves at a later stage," with participants pointing to supply shocks and AI-investment-boom demand as risks to entrenching inflation.
- Separately, Chair Kevin Warsh raised the idea of moving the FOMC from eight meetings a year to six (roughly every two months), to let more data accumulate between decisions.
- The Fed also announced five internal task forces on August 19 to examine aspects of monetary policy conduct (procedural/administrative, not a policy signal).

## 🎙️ Sentiment Read

**Net read: hawkish, and more hawkish than the July statement alone suggested.** The vote itself (9–3, hold) looked like a continuation of caution, but the minutes reveal the hawkish minority's reasoning circulated more broadly across the Committee than the vote count implied — "many participants," not just the three dissenters, saw tightening as likely necessary absent an inflation decline, and some flagged financial conditions as insufficiently restrictive. That's a meaningfully more hawkish read than a typical "unanimous-minus-a-few" hold.

Two other signals reinforce this: JPMorgan's Michael Feroli noted Chair Warsh's July press conference "raises questions about the new chair's credibility in delivering lower inflation," which could "add some urgency for the rest of the committee to act on its mandate" (commentary, not Fed communication). And Warsh's own suggestion of cutting meeting frequency from eight to six a year points toward a chair who wants fewer, more deliberate decision points rather than a preset easing or tightening path — consistent with his no-forward-guidance approach.

Working against the hawkish read: the weak July payrolls report (Aug 1/7) and cooling CPI/PCE trend have pulled market pricing toward "hold" as the base case, and Goldman Sachs is on record calling a September hike "very unlikely." The market is effectively caught between a hawkish committee-wide inflation debate on one hand and softening growth/labor data on the other — hence the ~65/35 hold/hike split rather than near-certainty either way.

- **Muted immediate reaction, but the setup has shifted hawkish at the margin.** The S&P 500 closed up only ~0.22% on August 19 despite the minutes' hawkish content — the modest gain was attributed more to the Treasury's debt-buyback announcement (which pulled yields down intraday) than to the Fed news itself, per CNBC/market-recap sources. That muted reaction may understate how the minutes reset the distribution of outcomes for September.

- **Long-end yield pressure is the more market-relevant story than the Fed funds rate itself right now.** The 30-year yield's push to a 19-year high (commentary: attributed by reporters to fiscal-deficit concerns, oil-driven inflation, and Warsh credibility questions) raises the discount rate applied to long-duration equity cash flows — a headwind historically felt hardest by high-multiple growth and tech names, even before the Fed moves its policy rate.

- **Rate-sensitive sectors (real estate, utilities, small-caps) face a two-sided setup:** commentary from strategists (e.g., State Street's Michael Arone, per reporting) points to an ongoing 2026 rotation toward value sectors (energy, materials, industrials, staples) on the view that small-caps and non-tech names are closing the earnings gap with mega-cap tech. But that thesis depends partly on rate cuts materializing; a hawkish-minutes-driven repricing toward "higher for longer" would work against the reflation/small-cap trade if it persists.

- **Growth vs. value:** the balance of evidence (hawkish minutes pulling one way, soft labor data and cooling core inflation pulling the other) argues for continued two-way volatility in the growth/value trade rather than a clean directional call. This is a market read from cited sources, not a prediction.

- **Bank/financials read is mixed:** a steeper 2s10s curve (~+52bp) is typically constructive for net interest margins, but rising long-end yields also raise banks' held-to-maturity securities losses and funding costs — a genuinely two-sided setup rather than a one-way tailwind.
